
\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

In this chapter, the results of the DA experiments are presented and discussed. We have one experiment ($H_s$ DA) where $H_s$ alone is assimilated, and one experiment (all vars DA) where $H_s, T_{m01}$ and $T_{m02}$ are assimilated together. The results of these experiments are compared to one another and to the forecast data with no DA applied. We will examine properties of the results such as the increments, RMSE and ensemble spread, as well the forecasts of the $H_s$ maximum on 12/03/2022.

Throughout this chapter, plots of $H_s$ and $T_{m01}$ are included. The plots of $T_{m02}$ all look very similar to the equivalent plots of $T_{m01}$, so they are not required for a discussion of the results. Hence they are not included in this chapter to aid readability. For completeness, they are included in appendix \ref{sec:t02plots} and may be referred to there.

We begin with a visual examination of timeseries to see qualitatively what effect the DA has had. Figure \ref{fig:buoymeanseries9} shows a timeseries of $H_s$ at the buoy from all vars DA, $H_s$ DA, no DA and the truth. This is taken from the 7-day forecast window starting at 12Z 09/03/2022. Observations from the first three days (the `assimilation window') of this forecast window are assimilated, and the end of this period is marked with a dotted blue line.

\label{fig:buoymeanseries9}
\end{figure}

The DA has little effect during the assimilation window, since the no DA forecast lies very close to the truth here. They diverge after the end of the assimilation window (at 3 days leadtime), and $H_s$ DA closely tracks the truth while all vars DA is closer to no DA. This is likely due to the effects of the different variables assimilated cancelling each other out. The four runs come back together at 4-5 days leadtime and all four diverge after 5 days leadtime.

These periods when the runs diverge reflect when we expect to see large forecast errors. 3-4 days leadtime contains the swell event which we have seen is associated with large forecast errors, and 5+ days leadtimes is at the end of forecast window when the error tends to be largest due to chaotic effects. That $H_s$ DA  matches the truth well at 3-4 days and not at 5+ days can be explained by the fact that 5+ days is further from the assimilation window. This means that any correction at 5+ days relies on cross-covariances in time, which are weak as seen in section \ref{sec:bgerrors}.

As well as the buoy location, we also examine the equivalent timeseries at a location far from the buoy. Figure \ref{fig:farmeanseries9} shows the same as \ref{fig:buoymeanseries9} at $30\degrees$ N  $80\degrees$ W, which is outside the Gulf of Mexico in the Atlantic ocean near Florida. Here there is no noticable improvement from DA, as all vars DA, $H_s$ DA and no DA all diverge from the truth by similar amounts. This is intuitively unsurprising since we would not expect observations at the buoy to be useful in predicting the waves so far away.

The increments (difference between DA and no DA) in figure \ref{fig:farmeanseries9} are quite large. It can also be seen that the different forecasts diverge greatly after the swell event at 3-4 days leadtime. This suggests that the DA system has successfully captured the dynamical chaos of the physical wave system.

\section{Increments}
\label{sec:increments}

The increments $\vect{d}^a_b = \x^a - \x_b$ are the corrections applied to the background state by the DA system (ensemble means). From equation \ref{eqn:cost3d}, the state that minimises the cost function will be close to the background when $\mat{P}^f$ is small compared to $\mat{R}$. This means that we expect the increments to be small in this situation. We have seen that generally $\mat{P}^f$ is small at locations far from the buoy, with some exceptions noted. %(and at times far from the assimilation window,)

From equation \ref{eqn:analysisupdate}, the increments are a linear function of the innovations $\vect{d}^o_b = \y - \mat{H}\x_b$. This means that the increments at the buoy location sould have the same sign as the innovations, since the forecast error variances $\mat{P}_{ii}$ are always positive. This is not necessarily true away from the buoy since the covariance between different locations may be negative. Furthermore, innovations at locations away from the buoy are unavailable since observations were only assimilated at the buoy.

However, due to the twin experiment method, the truth is available at all locations, so the increments can be compared to the negative forecast error $\vect{d}^t_b = \x_{true} - \x_b$. It is desirable for the increment to have the same sign as  $\vect{d}^t_b$, since this means that the DA has moved the analysis state closer to the truth than the forecast state.

Figure \ref{fig:hsinc} shows the increments in $H_s$ ensemble mean at 12Z 12/03/2022 taken from the 12Z 10/03/2022 forecast run. All vars DA has larger increments overall, and this is especially true far from the buoy. The increments from both DA runs match the sign of $\vect{d}^t_b$ somewhat well, but both have regions where the sign is incorrect.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width = .8\textwidth]{figures/hsinc12}
\caption{Significant wave height ensemble mean increments at 12Z 12/03/2022 from all vars DA (top left) and $H_s$ DA (top right), and significant wave height negative errors from the same time (bottom). Taken from 12Z 10/03/2022 forecast run. The buoy location is marked with a cross.}
\label{fig:hsinc}
\end{figure}

A notable example is the strong positive increment from all vars DA in the northern GoM, where $\vect{d}^t_b$ is slightly negative. The lower magnitude of increments in $H_s$ DA, especially near the buoy, means it undercorrects in some regions where the magnitude of $\vect{d}^t_b$ is large, such as just northwest  of the buoy in the western GoM. All vars DA does a better job at correcting for this large error.

Figure \ref{fig:t01inc} shows the same as \ref{fig:hsinc} but for the increments and negative errors in $T_{m01}$. The equivalent plots of $T_{m02}$ are shown in figure \ref{fig:t02inc}. All vars DA again show larger increments overall, and here all vars DA does a better job of matching the sign of innovation across the wider domain. It captures the strip of negative $\vect{d}^t_b$ seen extending through the central GoM, which is missed by $H_s$ DA. However just East of the buoy, $H_s$ DA has positive increments matching $\vect{d}^t_b$, while all vars DA is strongly negative.

Overall, all vars DA has generally larger increments across the whole domain and especially at locations far from the buoy. These  large, long-distance increments probably arise from the long distance covariances seen in section \ref{sec:bgerrors}. In that section, we concluded that some of the long distance covariances probably arise spuriously from sampling error, so these increments may be somewhat physically unrealistic. They could also be the result of long-distance correlations in the atmosphere that affect the wave model via forcing. Both DA experiments have increments which largely match the sign of the negative error $\vect{d}^t_b$, with all vars DA doing better overall in both variables.

\section{RMSE}
\label{sec:RMSE}

The root mean square error (RMSE) is a very important measure of the error in a forecast. It tells us how much the forecast value diverges from the truth over a given region or time window, and is overall a good measure for the accuracy of a forecast. Here we present the RMSE of the forecast as a function of both leadtime and space, and analyse it in the context of our knowledge of the DA increments and forecast error covariances.

Figure \ref{fig:rmsebyday} shows the RMSE of the ensemble mean from no DA, $H_s$ DA and all vars DA. The equivalent plot of $T_{m02}$ is shown in figure \ref{fig:t02rmsebyday}. The mean is taken over a 24-hour period from 12Z 12/03/2022 to 12Z 13/03/2022. This period was chosen because it contains the highest waves in the forecast period, which are associated with higher errors. Forecasts of this period are available from multiple start dates, giving a range of leadtimes. Errors are plotted for $H_s$ and $T_{m01}$. %$T_{m02}$ is not included as it has a very similar form to $T_{m01}$.

For $H_s$, during the assimilation window both $H_s$ DA and all vars DA show an improvement of  $\sim 60 \%$ over no DA. After the end of the window, the errors from all runs increase and at 3-4 days leadtime, $H_s$ DA shows a much greater RMSE reduction than all vars DA. At 4-5 days, $H_s$ DA has similar error to no DA and all vars DA shows an improvement of $\sim 50 \%$. 6-7 days is similar except the improvement from all vars DA is around 35 \%. At 5-6 days leadtime, both $H_s$ DA and all vars DA have higher RMSE than no DA. % why is this?

For $T_{m01}$ the results are similar, with consistent improvement from DA within the assimilation window and more variable results afterwards. All vars DA still performs better than $H_s$ DA with the exception of 1-2 days and 3-4 days leadtime. It is notable that $H_s$ DA gives improvements in this case since no observations of $T_{m01}$ are actually assimilated. This can be explained by the nonzero cross-covariance between $H_s$ and $T_{m01}$ (figure \ref{fig:covhst0112-0}). One difference between the errors in $T_{m01}$ and $H_s$ is that $H_s$ DA does slightly worse in predicting $T_{m01}$, when using the other two runs as a benchmark. This is unsurprising as we would expect assimilating $H_s$, $T_{m01}$ and $T_{m02}$ to give better predictions of $T_{m01}$ than $H_s$ alone.

Overall the performance of both DA experiments is similar during the assimilation window, and after the window all vars DA gives smaller RMSE than $H_s$ DA.

\section{Ensemble Spread}
\label{sec:ensspread}

Ensemble spread is an important measure of uncertainty in ensemble forecasts. One aim of this project is to reduce uncertainty in the forecasts so that they may be used to inform decisions with greater confidence. Figure \ref{fig:DAensspread} shows timeseries of ensemble standard deviation in $H_s$ and $T_{m01}$ at the buoy location from two different model runs. The equivalent plots of $T_{m02}$ are shown in figure \ref{fig:t02ensspread}. Both $H_s$ DA and all vars DA show a reduction in ensemble spread in both variables, as is expected from DA (section \ref{sec:DApractice}).

\label{fig:DAensspread}
\end{figure}

All vars DA shows a greater reduction in $T_{m01}$ ensemble spread overall. This is unsurprising since $T_{m01}$ is not directly assimilated in $H_s$ DA. In $H_s$ ensemble spread, both runs perform similarly overall, but $H_s$ DA generally shows lower spread during the assimilation window and the opposite is true after the assimilation window. This is likely because the observational error scale in $H_s$ was increased from $10\%$ in $H_s$ DA to $12\%$ in all vars DA, thus reducing the effect of the assimilation.

Figure \ref{fig:hsspreadmaps} shows the spatial distribution of $H_s$ ensemble spread at 12Z 11/03/2022, from 1 day and 5 days leadtime. At both leadtimes, All vars DA shows a greater reduction in ensemble spread than $H_s$ DA across most of the domain. The only exception to this is the vicinity of the buoy at 1 day leadtime, when $H_s$ DA shows a greater reduction. This is similar to what was seen in the timeseries, when $H_s$ DA had greater spread reduction outside of the assimilation window. This reflects the fact that the entire 7-day forecast window is updated in one step by the DA system, so the dimensions of space and time within a forecast window are treated equivalently. With this in mind, it is not surprising to see parallels in the spatial and temporal distributions of ensemble spread. The greatest reductions in spread are where the spread of no DA is greatest.

Overall, all vars DA shows greater reduction in ensemble spread at most times and locations, except near the buoy during the assimilation window. The reductions in spread are greatest where the no DA ensemble spread is greatest, which is good as this reduces the maximum uncertainty in the forecast.

It is not desirable to focus solely on reducing the ensemble spread, as this may lead to the ensemble being overconfident. As described in section \ref{sec:DApractice}, it is detrimental to the forecast to have ensemble spread smaller than the forecast errors as this can lead to too much confidence being placed in an inaccurate forecast. An example is the strong reduction in spread seen in the eastern GoM, where RMSE generally increases. To test the prevalence of this problem, the ensemble standard deviation is plotted against the RMSE.

Figure \ref{fig:spreaderr} shows a scatter plot of ensemble standard deviation against RMSE at the buoy location, for $H_s$ and $T_{m01}$. The equivalent plot of $T_{m02}$ is shown in figure \ref{fig:t02spreaderr}. RMSE is calculated from the ensemble mean and averaged over a 6 hour rolling window in leadtime. Data from all forecast windows from $H_s$ DA and all vars DA is included, with $H_s$ DA represented by circles and all vars DA represented by triangles. The leadtime of each data point within its forecast window is shown by its colour. Lines of best fit are calculated for both $H_s$ DA and all vars DA, and plotted along with the 1:1 line.

These are both much greater than in the forecast prior to DA. The colours on the figure show that most of the occasions where RMSE is significantly more than the spread are late in the forecast window (leadtime > 4 days) with few exceptions.

So $H_s$ DA gives more overconfident $H_s$ ensembles and all vars DA gives more overconfident $T_{m01}$ ensembles, with $H_s$ DA giving more overconfident ensembles overall. The high prevalence of overconfident $T_{m01}$ ensembles from all vars DA lines up with the decreased spread in $T_{m01}$ from all vars DA seen in figure \protect\ref{fig:DAensspread}, and may be due to the increased reduction in ensemble spread from multiple variables being assimilated. %I was not able to find an explanation for the high prevalence of overconfident $H_s$ ensembles from $H_s$ DA, and further work is needed to understand this. Overconfident ensembles are most important to avoid when the error is large, since larger forecast errors present greater risk for incorrect decision making. So the count of the overconfident ensembles was repeated with a minimum threshold that the RMSE must meet in order for the ensemble to be counted. These thresholds are 0.1 m for $H_s$ and 0.2 s for $T_{m01}$, and were set at values that judged to be acceptable levels of error in each variable. After applying these thresholds, the frequency of overconfident ensembles reduced by at least 50\% in all cases except in the $T_{m01}$ ensembles from $H_s$ DA. This reflects the fact that the errors in $T_{m01}$ from $H_s$ DA are large. This now gives overconfidence prevalences of 10-20\% in most cases.

In summary, the DA has reduced the ensemble spread as expected, with all vars DA having a stronger effect then $H_s$ DA. The greatest reductions in spread occur where the pre-DA spread is greatest. Both DA experiments increase the prevalence of overconfident ensembles, but the ensemble spread is still less than the RMSE in general, so it is judged to still be a good measure of uncertainty.

\section{Errors in Maximum $H_s$}
\label{sec:hsmax}

$H_s$ maxima are of particular interest in this project as they present the most danger to offshore operations. In this section we examine the effect of DA on the errors in the timing and magnitude of the $H_s$ maximum on 12/03/2022. Figure \ref{fig:peakerrors} shows scatter plots of the timing and magnitude from no DA, $H_s$ DA and all vars DA, relative to the truth. The maximum is selected from the period spanning 12Z 12/03/2022 to 12Z 13/03/2022. The left plot is generated from the model run starting 12Z 10/03/2022 and the right plot is generated from the model run starting 12Z 08/03/2022, giving leadtime ranges of 2-3 days and 4-5 days respectively. Table \ref{tab:hsmax} shows the ensemble RMSE in timing and magnitude from each ensemble for both runs.

Note on the scatter plots that the points are arranged discretely at integer numbers of hours: this is simply because the forecast data used has a time resolution of one hour. This could reduce the maximum significant wave height detected if the true maximum lies in the middle of two timesteps. One way around this may be to fit a quadratic polynomial to the model data points either side of the peak and find the maximum value of this polynomial, but this was not explored here. The forecasters at Fugro work regularly with hourly forecast data without any interpolation applied, and we assume they are aware that this can lead to inaccurate maximum values being sampled.

At a leadtime of 2-3 days, there is a large improvement in forecasts of timing and magnitude from both DA runs. From the scatter plot, the ensemble spread is considerably tighter around the true value, and the RMSE in both quantities is reduced. All vars DA does slightly better than $H_s$ DA in predicting the timing of the maximum, and vice versa for the magnitude, but the differences between the two are small compared to the difference of both with no DA.

After 4-5 days, the errors are larger in all quantities but the magnitude from the no DA run. The differences between the two DA runs is larger, as $H_s$ DA has a similar error in timing to no DA, but shows a small improvement in the magnitude of 13\%. Conversely, all vars DA has similar error in magnitude to no DA, but shows an improvement in timing of 35\%.

Overall, $H_s$ DA and all vars DA perform similarly to each other during the assimilation window, and both significantly outperform no DA as expected during this time. As seen in other aspects, their performance differs more after the assimilation window, with $H_s$ DA providing better forecasts of the magnitude of the peak and all vars DA providing better forecasts of its timing.
