\section{The need for Reliable Wave Forecasting}

Offshore operations form an important part of many industries and contribute to the economies of coastal countries round the world. Some examples are offshore wind farms in the renewable energy industry, and undersea cable maintenance in telecommunications. Ocean waves pose a significant risk to offshore operations as they can lead to the loss of life and damage to equipment \citep{Adumene2022,Ren2021}. This means that accurate wave predictions are a critical safety issue, as offshore operations can only be carried out safely when the waves are sufficiently calm.

To provide these predictions, the MetOcean Forecasting division of Fugro produces wave forecasts for a client undertaking offshore operations in the Gulf of Mexico. It is important to understand the risk posed by waves in this region since increased cyclone intensity is leading to increased extreme wave heights in the Gulf of Mexico \citep{Appendini2014}. As well as needing to be accurate, it is important to know the uncertainty in these wave forecasts as the unnecesary pausing of operations is very costly, and knowledge of uncertainty can help to avoid and mitigate this cost.

To this end, Fugro produce ensemble forecasts, so that the ensemble spread can be used to infer uncertainty. As the production of ensemble forecasts is more costly than a single deterministic forecast, these ensemble forecasts are produced only when extreme conditions are expected, such as during winter storms. The rest of the time, a single deterministic forecast is produced instead. They produce non-ensemble forecasts year-round and ensemble forecasts are produced when extreme conditions are expected, such as during winter storms.

To account for uncertainty in the deterministic forecast, Fugro use a method called a site-specific alpha factor. This is based on the alpha-factor method developed by Det Norske Veritas \citep{DNV2011} and further expanded upon by \citet{Gintautas2016,Wilcken2012,Wu2021}. This method seeks to apply corrections to the forecast by defining the so-called alpha factor

\begin{equation}
\alpha = \frac{H_\text{max}}{H_\text{max,cor}},
\end{equation}

where $H_\text{max}$ is the wave height with a $10^{-4}$ probability of exceedance during a given window. $H_\text{max,cor}$ is the wave height with the same exceedance probability during the same window, after the forecast is corrected for bias and uncertainty. Following \cite{Liu1998}, the Rayleigh distribution is used to model the probability distribution of maximum wave height over a window as follows:

\begin{equation}
\prob (H \leq H_\text{max}) = \left[ 1 - e^{\left( -2 \frac{H^2}{H_s^2} \right)} \right]^\frac{t}{T_p}.
\label{eqn:rayleigh}
\end{equation}

Here $H_s$ is the significant wave height during the window, $t$ is the length of the window and $T_p$ is the peak period during the window. $H_\text{max}$ is then chosen so that $1 - \prob (H \leq H_\text{max}) = 10^{-4}$. $H_\text{max,cor}$ is found by integrating the joint probability density function of $H$ and $H_s$,

\begin{equation}
\prob (H,H_s) = \prob (H|H_s) \prob (H_s),
\end{equation}

where $\prob (H|H_s)$ is the Rayleigh distribution from equation \ref{eqn:rayleigh} and $\prob (H_s)$ is assumed to be a normal distribution with mean equal to the bias corrected forecast value of $H_s$ and standard deviation equal to the forecast standard deviation. The bias and standard deviation were calculated from the timeseries of model output and observations spanning from September 2020 to May 2021. Integrating $\prob (H,H_s)$ over all values of $H_s$ gives a corrected distribution of maximum wave height during the window which can be used to define $H_\text{max,cor}$ in the same way as $H_\text{max}$.

Once the value of $\alpha$ is found, it is used to scale the $H_s$ model output via $H_{s,\text{cor}} = \frac{H_s}{\alpha}$. So if the model predicts $H_s = 1.5$m and $\alpha = 0.75$, the scaled forecast value is $H_{s,\text{cor}} = 2$m. Qualitatively speaking, $\alpha$ will always be less than 1, since forecast uncertainty acts to broaden the distribution of maximum wave height. This means that $H_\text{max,cor}$ is always more than $H_\text{max}$, as illustrated in figure \ref{fig:Hcordist}.

Fugro has conducted research into the performance of the site-specific alpha factor method, but has not made the report publicly available. However, from personal correspondence with the Fugro metocean team \citep{Nikolova2024}, we learned that this method is overly cautious and systematically produces significant wave heights above those seen in the observational record. This conclusion was reached after examining the performance of the method in five strong storms between January and March 2022.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width = .6\textwidth]{figures/Hcordist}
	\caption{Schematic distributions of maximum wave height in a window, uncorrected (solid) and corrected for forecast bias and uncertainty (dashed).}
	\label{fig:Hcordist}
\end{figure}

As well as producing these forecasts, Fugro has a buoy based near to the client which produces hourly observations of the sea state. Data assimilation (DA) refers to a range of techniques used to integrate observations into a forecast, with the aim of making the forecast more accurate. In this project, the potential of DA to reduce uncertainty and error in these wave forecasts is explored. The aim is to develop a DA method which gives better forecasts than the alpha factor method currently in use.

\section{Previous work on DA for Wave Forecasts}
\label{sec:prevwork}

Several studies have been completed previously investigating the effectiveness of DA methods to improve wave forecasts. In this section, some of these studies and their findings are summarised, and the differences between this study and previous ones are discussed.

\citet{Aouf2006} assimilated spectral wave parameters into forecasts produced using the WAM model \citep{Gunther1992}. The DA method they used is called optimal interpolation (OI) and produces an analysis field which is a weighted sum of the forecast pre-DA (called the background in DA terminology) and the difference between the background and the observations (called the innovations). The weights are determined by the estimated errors in the background state and observations, with a more detailed description given in section 2(b) of \cite{Aouf2006}.

They did not assimilate real observations but rather pseudo-observations generated from model data, since the study was intended to investigate the impact of a proposed new observation system. This method is called a twin experiment or observing system simulation experiment (OSSE) and is commonly used for this purpose since it does not require any observational data \citep{Zeng2020}. In this study we also perform a twin experiment due to another advantage of the methodology: that the model data representing the truth is available at all times and locations, which allows for a more thorough evaluation of forecast errors. More details on this aspect of the method are given in section \ref{sec:twinexp}.

\cite{Caires2018} investigated the effect on wave forecasts of using a DA method called the ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF) \citep{Evensen2003} to adjust the wind fields used as forcing in a wave model. They used hourly observations of the 10-metre wind from buoys at six different locations. Their results are shown in figure \ref{fig:caires2018fig30}. The RMSE with DA at two locations is plotted against forecast leadtime, and the forecast RMSE is compared to the RMSE at 0h leadtime with and without DA (referred to in the figure as hindcast and analysis respectively).

An improvement can be seen in forecasts of significant wave height and peak period, but it is relatively small compared to other studies examined here \citep{Houghton2022,Houghton2023} and short lived, not extending past 24hrs in any instance. This may be due to the indirect relationship between the assimilated wind data and the forecasted wave variables. In this study, observations of the forecasted wave variables are assimilated directly, and this is expected to lead to improved performance over the wind-based approach.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{figures/caires2018fig30}
\caption{RMSE in significant wave height (left) and peak period (right) against forecast leadtime from two different observation locations. Figure 30 from \citet{Caires2018}.}
\label{fig:caires2018fig30}
\end{figure}

Another possible reason for the relatively poor performance in this case is the small number of observations used. Observations were available hourly from only six different locations, while operational DA systems commonly assimilate on the order of $10^7$ observations at a time \citep{Bannister2017}. Our study is also limited in this regard, as data is only available from one buoy. A study which used many more observations is \cite{Houghton2022}, in which an optimal interpolation (OI) method \citep{Komen1994} was used to compare the effects of assimilating significant wave height and spectra. In this study, observations came from the global Sofar spotter buoy network \citep{Raghukumar2019} which contains over 600 buoys. A plot of their results is shown in figure \ref{fig:houghton2022fig3}.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{Figures/houghton2022fig3}
\caption{RMSE against forecast leadtime from spectral DA and significant wave height ($H_s$) DA, normalised by the RMSE without DA. Results shown for significant wave height $H_s$, peak period $T_p$, mean period $T_m$, directional spread $\sigma_\theta$, peak direction $D_p$ and mean direction $D_m$. Figure 3 from \citet{Houghton2022}.}
\label{fig:houghton2022fig3}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:houghton2022fig3} shows that both variables give very similar improvements in predictions of significant wave height, while spectral assimilation gives better results for other quantities such as period and direction. They also found that the spectral DA did particularly well in swell-dominated fields when compared to assimilating significant wave height. This shows that the type of observations assimilated can have a large effect on the performance of a DA system.

In this project $H_s$ is assimilated along with two measures of wave period, $T_{m01}$ and $T_{m02}$. These variables are related to different moments of the spectrum (see equations \ref{eqn:moments} - \protect\ref{eqn:mainvars}), so assimilating both $T_{m01}$ and $T_{m02}$ as well as significant wave height should have some of the benefits of spectral DA identified in \cite{Houghton2022}. Since assimilating a full wave spectrum involves much more data than bulk variables such as significant wave height and period, the method used here is expected to incur less computational cost than assimilating the full spectrum.

To investigate the effect of the DA method used, \citet{Houghton2023} assimilated significant wave height observations from satellite altimeters and Sofar spotter buoys. They compared two different DA methods, OI and the ensemble-based local ensemble transform Kalman filter (LETKF) \citep{Hunt2007}. Plots of their results are included in figure \ref{fig:houghton2023fig6}.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width = .8\textwidth]{figures/houghton2023fig6}
\caption{Forecast bias (left) and RMSE (right) from altimeter observations (top) and buoy observations (bottom), plotted against forecast leadtime. Figure 6 from \citet{Houghton2023}.}
\label{fig:houghton2023fig6}
\end{figure}

They found that both methods reduced forecast error and bias out to 2.5 days lead time, with LETKF having slightly greater forecast skill overall. In addition to this, they found that the LETKF gave more physically realistic increments due to its flow dependent background error covariance matrix. A plot showing increments from each method is included in figure \ref{fig:houghton2023fig3}. This shows that as well as the observations, the DA method used affects DA performance.

The method used in this project borrows the analysis step from the ensemble transform Kalman filter (ETKF) method (see section \ref{sec:kalmanfilters}), which is similar to the LETKF except it does not use localisation and so allows observations to impact the state over long distances. Further discussion on the topic of localisation is included in section \ref{sec:DApractice}. This method was chosen over LETKF partly because it leads to a simpler DA implementation, and partly because the limited scope of the available observations means that localisation would stop the DA from making any change to the state across most of the domain. A major advantage of LETKF is its computational efficiency \citep{Hunt2007}, and borrowing techniques from the ETKF should also lead to an efficient method.

One other notable aspect of \citet{Houghton2023} is their diagnosis of the errors in the buoy observations. They used a colocation study to estimate the error standard deviation as a function of observed significant wave height, and found a strong linear relationship. As we are also using buoy observations, we expect that this linear relationship will also exist in our observations, so this method will be used to estimate the observation error variance. Further details are given in section \ref{sec:scaleerrs}.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width = .8\textwidth]{figures/houghton2023fig3}
\caption{An example significant wave height increment from both LETKF and OI methods. Colours show value of increment, markers show the locations of observation buoys. Figure 3 from \citet{Houghton2023}.}
\label{fig:houghton2023fig3}
\end{figure}

In summary, observations of $H_s$, $T_{m01}$ and $T_{m02}$ are assimilated in order to capture the benefits of spectral DA identified in \citet{Houghton2022} while avoiding the computational cost. Our DA method borrows from the ETKF in the interests of simplicity and computational efficiency, and also to allow DA to update the state at all locations in the domain. The twin experiment methodology is used to allow for more thorough investigation of forecast errors. Following \cite{Houghton2023}, a colocation study is used to estimate the observation error variance.

In this project, we apply DA to a specific forecasting context and client in the Gulf of Mexico, and so we are constrained by the observation data available in this context. As seen above, the studies with more observations saw better DA performance, and our results should be evaluated with this limitation in mind. Another limitation which none of the previous studies were subject to is that we are unable to run the wave model ourselves, which is a central step of most DA systems. We expect that the knowledge gained from these previous studies will allow us to get good results in spite of these limitations.

\section{Aims of the Project}
\label{sec:aim}

The main research question in this project is ``\textbf{What kind of reduction in forecast error can be achieved by assimilating $H_s$, $T_{m01}$ and $T_{m02}$ into ocean wave forecasts?}''

To this end, we perform a twin experiment using a DA method which borrows from the ensemble transform Kalman filter (ETKF) to assimilate observations of the three variables. A twin experiment is a DA experiment in which fictional observations are generated and assimilated in place of real observations (see section \ref{sec:twinexp} for details). We perform one experiment (all vars DA) in which all three aforementioned variables are assimilated, and one experiment ($H_s$ DA) in which significant wave height alone is assimilated. The reasoning for this is that extreme wave heights are of particular interest to Fugro's client due to their impact on operational safety, so we consider $H_s$ to be the most important variable of the three. We examine the results of these two experiments in comparison with each other, and with the forecast data before DA, to evaluate the performance of DA in both instances.

Various aspects of the results will be examined to gain a full understanding of the DA performance. These are:

\begin{itemize}
\item Increments. The changes made to the forecast by the DA system are called increments, and they can aid understanding of how DA is modifying the model state. We hypothesise that the increments will realistically reflect the information contained in the observations and act to move the forecast state closer to the truth. This is examined in section \ref{sec:increments}.
\item The root mean square error (RMSE) of the ensemble mean. We hypothesise that the DA will reduce RMSE both at the buoy and away from the buoy to a lesser extent. This is examined in section \ref{sec:RMSE}.
%\item The correlation between ensemble mean and the true state. DA should make the ensemble mean more closely correlated with the true state, so we will test this hypothesis.
\item Ensemble spread. This is a commonly used indicator of uncertainty in ensemble forecasts, which can give an estimate of forecast error and allow a forecast to inform decisions with greater or lesser confidence. It is desirable then to have ensemble spread which is comparable with the forecast error so that this estimate is a good one. We hypothesise that DA will reduce ensemble spread, but not so much that it ceases to be a good measure of forecast error. This is examined in section \ref{sec:ensspread}.
\item Errors in maximum $H_s$. Extremes of $H_s$ are of particular interest in these forecasts as these are the events which cause danger and require the pausing of offshore operations. We hypothesise that the DA will reduce error in the timings and magnitudes of significant wave height maxima. This is examined in section \ref{sec:hsmax}.
\end{itemize}
